## New Items

TITLE: New Hidden Text Found on Previously "Blank" Dead Sea Scroll Fragments (Manchester Collection)
CATEGORY: transmission
EXPLANATION: In 2026, Professor Joan E. Taylor (King's College London), working with Professor Marcello Fidanzio (Faculty of Theology of Lugano) and Dr. Dennis Mizzi (University of Malta) as part of a Leverhulme Trust-funded study and the Network for the Study of Dispersed Qumran Cave Artefacts and Archival Sources, re-examined 51 Dead Sea Scroll fragments held at the University of Manchester that had long been catalogued as blank. Using multispectral imaging — a technique that reveals ink invisible to the naked eye — the team identified faint Hebrew and Aramaic writing on four of the fragments. The most substantial preserves four lines totaling roughly 15–16 legible letters, including a clear instance of the word "Shabbat" (Sabbath); researchers have tentatively floated a possible connection to Ezekiel 46:1–3 (a passage on Sabbath temple observance), though this specific textual identification is provisional and the full analysis is described as a forthcoming publication rather than already peer-reviewed in print. The finding is a concrete, recent (2026) example of how modern imaging technology continues to recover previously invisible manuscript evidence from the Qumran corpus, directly bearing on the project's textual-transmission theme — it adds another witness fragment to the material record even as its precise content (the Ezekiel identification) remains unconfirmed pending formal publication.
SOURCE: King's College London news release, "Dead Sea Scroll fragments thought to be blank reveal text" (2026), reporting on a Leverhulme Trust-funded study led by Prof. Joan E. Taylor (KCL) with Prof. Marcello Fidanzio (Lugano) and Dr. Dennis Mizzi (Malta); independently corroborated by the University of Manchester's own announcement and by Smithsonian Magazine, ScienceAlert, and Sci.News coverage of the same story.
URL: https://www.kcl.ac.uk/news/dead-sea-scroll-fragments-thought-to-be-blank-reveal-text

TITLE: Hornkohl's Linguistic Case for a Persian-Period (Late) Dating of Jonah — Contested Counter-Evidence
CATEGORY: linguistics
EXPLANATION: In a peer-reviewed 2026 article in the journal *Religions*, Cambridge Hebraist Dr. Aaron D. Hornkohl surveys more than fifty linguistic features in the book of Jonah that various scholars have proposed as markers of "late" Biblical Hebrew. He finds eleven to be strong indicators of lateness, with many of the rest showing only partial or ambiguous significance, often better explained by genre, deliberate style-switching, or contact with Phoenician/Aramaic. His conclusion is that, cumulatively, the evidence places Jonah's Hebrew most plausibly in the sixth-to-fifth-century BCE Persian Period — centuries after the eighth-century-BCE setting of the historical prophet Jonah ben Amittai referenced in 2 Kings 14:25 — though he notes that affinities with Rabbinic Hebrew complicate a precise placement. This is flagged here explicitly as a serious, methodologically careful piece of mainstream academic scholarship that argues for a *late* compositional date for a Tanach book on internal linguistic grounds, rather than corroborating early dating. It should not be folded into the database's existing "early dating" linguistics entries (e.g., the Torah-focused archaic-pronominal-suffix and Wayyiqtol/vav-consecutive entries), since it concerns Jonah specifically, not the Pentateuch, and its conclusion runs the opposite direction. The broader question of whether linguistic dating of Biblical Hebrew reliably distinguishes early from late composition remains an active, unresolved scholarly controversy (a point the paper itself engages with), and this item should be understood in that contested context rather than as a settled result.
SOURCE: Aaron D. Hornkohl, "Hebrew Diachrony and Jonah: The Periodization of a Linguistically Anomalous Work," *Religions* 17, no. 7 (2026): article 754, Special Issue "Texts, Language, and Style in Biblical Literature, the Semitic Literatures of the Ancient Near East, and Post-Biblical Literature." Author affiliation independently confirmed: Aaron D. Hornkohl, University Associate Professor in Hebrew, Faculty of Asian and Middle Eastern Studies, University of Cambridge (Girton College).
URL: https://doi.org/10.3390/rel17070754
