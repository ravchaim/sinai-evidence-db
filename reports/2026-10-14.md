TITLE: The Yedaiah Bulla — A Possible Seal of King Josiah's Court Official Asaiah
CATEGORY: history_corrob
EXPLANATION: In July 2025 the Temple Mount Sifting Project (TMSP) announced a tiny clay bulla (seal impression), recovered from soil removed from the Temple Mount decades ago and sifted by archaeologist Mordechai Ehrlich, reading "Yed[a‛]yah (son of) Asayahu." Based on the letter forms, researchers date the seal to the late seventh or early sixth century BCE. "Asayahu" is the fuller form of the name "Asaiah," one of the officials King Josiah sent to consult the prophetess Huldah after the discovery of the Torah scroll in the Temple (2 Kings 22:12; 2 Chronicles 34:20). TMSP researchers consider it "highly plausible" that the bulla belonged to this official's son. The Biblical Archaeology Society's own coverage is more cautious, stating it is impossible to conclusively prove the bulla refers to the biblical official, though the find does confirm the name Asayahu was in current use in Judah's royal administration in the right period. As with the other named-official bullae already in this database (Nathan-Melech, Jehucal, Gedaliah son of Pashhur, etc.), the evidentiary weight is that a name attested in the biblical narrative turns up as a real, contemporaneously used administrative name in the right stratum — not a certain identification of the specific individual named in the text.
SOURCE: Temple Mount Sifting Project announcement, July 30, 2025 ("Has the Name of King Josiah's Trusted Official Been Found on an Ancient Sealing?"); reported in The Times of Israel and by the Biblical Archaeology Society ("Bulla Inscribed with Biblical Name Discovered by Temple Mount Sifting Project").
URL: https://www.timesofisrael.com/tiny-2600-year-old-clay-sealing-inscribed-with-biblical-name-found-in-temple-mount-soil/

TITLE: "Three New Kings of Assyria" — A Methodological Caveat on the Assyrian King List
CATEGORY: meta
EXPLANATION: A 2026 study by Alexander Johannes Edmonds (University of Münster) and Eckart Frahm (Yale University), "Three New Kings of Assyria," published in the Journal of Cuneiform Studies (vol. 78, 2026; DOI 10.1086/741239), identifies three short-reigned Assyrian kings — a previously unknown Tiglath-pileser (c. 763–762 BCE), a Shalmaneser (c. 747–745 BCE), and an Aššur-uballiṭ (c. 913–912 BCE) — none of whom appear on the canonical Assyrian King List. The authors reconstruct their existence from administrative and dedicatory texts, including a royal land-grant document and a list of kings who dedicated silver vessels to the god Ashur, and argue each reigned less than two years and was likely omitted from the official King List by a successor. This is included not as a "proof" but as an honest caveat: this database cites several Assyrian royal inscriptions and king-lists (the Black Obelisk, the Annals of Tiglath-Pileser III, Esarhaddon's Prism, etc.) to corroborate biblical royal chronology, and this peer-reviewed study is a reminder, from mainstream Assyriology itself, that even the best-preserved ancient Near Eastern chronological king-lists can be selectively edited and incomplete. That caution cuts in both directions and should temper overconfidence in any single ancient chronological source, biblical or otherwise. Per Archaeology Magazine's coverage, the claim is new and "not yet universally accepted" by other specialists.
SOURCE: Alexander Johannes Edmonds and Eckart Frahm, "Three New Kings of Assyria," Journal of Cuneiform Studies 78 (2026), DOI: 10.1086/741239; reported in Yale News (July 9, 2026) and Archaeology Magazine (August 4, 2026).
URL: https://news.yale.edu/2026/07/09/watch-throne-uncovering-three-new-kings-assyria
