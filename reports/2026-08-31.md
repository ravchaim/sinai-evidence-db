TITLE: The Tel Ḥadid Mother-of-Pearl Seal — A Moon-God Emblem in Assyrian-Period Israel
CATEGORY: archaeology
EXPLANATION: A peer-reviewed article by Ido Koch (Tel Aviv University) and colleagues (Leibin-Graiver, Bar-Yosef Mayer, Ranzer, Wrathall, Myers, and Parker), "A Unique Mother-of-Pearl Stamp Seal from Late Iron Age Tel Ḥadid, Israel," published in 2026 in the journal Levant (Council for British Research in the Levant / Taylor & Francis), reports a small (under 2 cm) stamp seal recovered from a securely dated seventh-century BCE refuse pit at Tel Ḥadid in central Israel, in a stratigraphic context following the Assyrian conquest of the region. It is the first known stamp seal from the entire southern Levant carved from mother-of-pearl — identified as Pinctada margaritifera, a pearl oyster native to the Indo-Pacific, thousands of kilometers from Israel — attesting to long-distance exchange networks reaching the region under Assyrian rule. The seal's engraved imagery is a crescent-on-standard emblem, a recognized variant of the symbol of Sin, the moon god whose principal cult center was at Harran in northern Mesopotamia; this emblem is well attested on seals and reliefs across the Neo-Assyrian world in the 8th–7th centuries BCE. For the Sinai Project's themes, the find is a genuine, physically excavated data point corroborating the religious environment the Hebrew Bible repeatedly polemicizes against during the Assyrian-vassal period — the "host of heaven"/astral worship condemned in texts such as 2 Kings 21:3–5, 23:5, Deuteronomy 4:19, Jeremiah 8:2, and Zephaniah 1:5 — by showing that specifically Assyrian/Harranian lunar-cult iconography was materially present in Judah's cultural orbit at exactly the period those biblical polemics describe. It should not be overstated: the seal was found at Tel Ḥadid (in the Assyrian administrative province of Samerina, not Judah proper), and it demonstrates the presence and circulation of this iconography in the region rather than proving any specific individual's beliefs or practice.
SOURCE: Ido Koch, N. Leibin-Graiver, D. Bar-Yosef Mayer, O. Ranzer, K. Wrathall, N. Myers, and S. T. Parker, "A Unique Mother-of-Pearl Stamp Seal from Late Iron Age Tel Ḥadid, Israel," Levant (2026), pp. 1–17
URL: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00758914.2026.2663758

TITLE: "Cosmological Fine-Tuning: The View from 2025" — A Contested Philosophical Survey of Design, Multiverse, and Limited-God Explanations
CATEGORY: philosophy
EXPLANATION: Geraint F. Lewis (astrophysicist, University of Sydney), Luke A. Barnes (astrophysicist, Western Sydney University), and Philip Goff (philosopher, Durham University), "Cosmological fine-tuning: the view from 2025," published in Religious Studies (Cambridge University Press) vol. 62, pp. S100–S110 (received June 2025, published online October 2025), surveys the current state of the fine-tuning debate: the observation, uncontested among the authors, that the universe's physical constants and initial conditions appear fine-tuned within an extremely narrow range for the existence of life, and that slightly different values would render the universe sterile. The paper's substantive contribution is philosophical rather than a new physical finding — it works through the main rival explanations of that fine-tuning (traditional theism, the multiverse hypothesis, and a "pantheistic God of limited power"), and engages a leading objection to the multiverse-based explanation, that inferring a multiverse from fine-tuning commits an "inverse gambler's fallacy." For the Sinai Project's cosmology and philosophy themes, this is useful primarily as evidence that the fine-tuning question remains a live, actively debated topic in current mainstream philosophy of religion and physics — not as a citation that itself concludes in favor of the Torah's theism. The paper does not adjudicate between the three explanations it presents, and one of its co-authors (Goff) is a proponent of a non-classical, "limited" panpsychist-adjacent conception of God elsewhere in his work — so this source should be cited as evidence that fine-tuning is a serious, unresolved, mainstream academic question with theism as one live contender, not as a source that itself endorses the Torah's specific conception of G-d.
SOURCE: Geraint F. Lewis, Luke A. Barnes, and Philip Goff, "Cosmological fine-tuning: the view from 2025," Religious Studies 62 (2025): S100–S110, doi: 10.1017/S0034412525101170
URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/religious-studies/article/cosmological-finetuning-the-view-from-2025/E134326EB1A48C040F593BDAC266AFC2
