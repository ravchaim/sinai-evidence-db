TITLE: The Asayahu Bulla — A Possible Trace of King Josiah's Servant
CATEGORY: archaeology
EXPLANATION: In July 2025 the Temple Mount Sifting Project (sifting soil removed from the Temple Mount, directed by Gabriel Barkay and Zachi Dvira/Zweig) announced a First Temple-period clay bulla (seal impression), paleographically dated to the late 7th–early 6th century BCE, read by epigraphers Anat Mendel-Geberovich and Zachi Dvira as "Belonging to Yed[a']yah (son of) Asayahu." A royal official named Asaiah/Asayahu who served King Josiah is mentioned twice in Tanach (2 Kings 22:12; 2 Chronicles 34:20). The project's own announcement is appropriately cautious: because the bulla came from disturbed, sifted soil rather than a controlled stratigraphic context, and because the name Asayahu was evidently in circulation (a similarly-inscribed but unprovenanced bulla naming an "Asayahu, servant of the king" surfaced on the antiquities market roughly twenty years ago, with authenticity that cannot be independently confirmed), it cannot be shown that this is the same individual named in the biblical text. What it does establish is that the specific personal name attested in the Josiah narrative was in actual administrative use in Judah at the time the text places it — the same evidentiary pattern (a biblical name recovered in situ from the right period and place) already represented elsewhere in this database by entries such as the Nathan-Melech Seal and the Ophel Bulla of Hezekiah, but for a different, previously uncovered official's name.
SOURCE: Temple Mount Sifting Project (tmsifting.org), July 30, 2025; corroborated by Associates for Biblical Research, Times of Israel, and JNS
URL: https://tmsifting.org/en/2025/07/30/asayahu/

TITLE: Linguistic Dating of Jonah Reassessed — A Contested Late-Period Argument
CATEGORY: linguistics
EXPLANATION: A 2026 peer-reviewed article by Aaron D. Hornkohl (University of Cambridge), "Hebrew Diachrony and Jonah: The Periodization of a Linguistically Anomalous Work" (Religions 17.7, article 754), revisits the century-and-a-half-old debate — going back to E. B. Pusey's 1860 defense of an early date — over whether the Book of Jonah's unusual vocabulary and grammar indicate a late (post-exilic) composition. Hornkohl surveys more than fifty features previously flagged as "late," finding eleven that hold up as reasonably strong indicators of lateness while many others turn out to be explicable by genre, style-switching, or contact with Phoenician/Aramaic rather than by date; his cumulative assessment is that Jonah's Hebrew most plausibly reflects a late stratum, tentatively the 6th–5th century BCE Persian period, though he notes affinities with Rabbinic Hebrew that complicate a precise placement. This is directly relevant to the project's "linguistics" layer, which elsewhere argues from archaic linguistic features (e.g. the Song of the Sea, Ha'azinu) toward early dating of specific Torah passages using the same general methodology (diachronic Hebrew linguistics). Two caveats are important: (1) linguistic dating of Biblical Hebrew remains a genuinely contested methodology within the field — other scholars attribute Jonah's unusual language to dialect or literary artifice rather than date, and this database's own entries on archaic poetry rely on the same contested method to argue the opposite direction (early dating) for different texts; and (2) this study concerns the Book of Jonah specifically, not the Torah/Pentateuch, and its conclusion (a late date for Jonah) is in tension with, not supportive of, an early/traditional dating of Jonah. It is included here as an example of genuine, current, contested scholarship on a topic central to the project's linguistics layer, not as evidence for any particular side of that debate.
SOURCE: Aaron D. Hornkohl, "Hebrew Diachrony and Jonah: The Periodization of a Linguistically Anomalous Work," Religions 17, no. 7 (2026): article 754
URL: https://doi.org/10.3390/rel17070754
