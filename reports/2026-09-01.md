## One item survived verification

TITLE: AI-Assisted Word-Frequency Analysis Statistically Distinguishes Three Scribal Traditions in the Enneateuch

CATEGORY: literary

EXPLANATION: In June 2025, an international team — Shira Faigenbaum-Golovin (Duke University), Alon Kipnis (Reichman University), Axel Bühler (Institut protestant de théologie de Paris), Eli Piasetzky (Tel Aviv University), Thomas Römer (Collège de France), and Israel Finkelstein (University of Haifa) — published "Critical biblical studies via word frequency analysis: Unveiling text authorship" in *PLOS ONE* (20(6): e0322905). Using AI-assisted statistical modeling sensitive to small deviations in the frequency of common, non-content-bearing words (rather than rare vocabulary or theme words), the study analyzed the first nine books of the Hebrew Bible (the Enneateuch, Genesis–Kings) and reported that the texts cluster into three statistically distinguishable groups corresponding, broadly, to the priestly material within the Torah, the book of Deuteronomy, and the Deuteronomistic History (Joshua–Kings). This result tracks classical source-critical (Documentary-Hypothesis-style) divisions rather than a single undifferentiated authorial voice across those nine books. This bears directly — and in real tension — on this project's own entries arguing for the Torah's literary and linguistic unity (e.g. Cassuto's unity argument against source fragmentation, and the argument that divine-name distribution is contextual rather than source-based): it is a genuine, peer-reviewed, mainstream-academic quantitative finding that lends statistical support to composite, multi-tradition authorship of the Pentateuch and the historical books that follow it, and it should be weighed honestly rather than omitted. Two caveats belong alongside it: first, the authors present the method as a redaction-critical tool for characterizing textual groupings, not as a resolution of the theological question of Mosaic authorship, which the statistical method cannot itself adjudicate; second, whether word-frequency clustering reflects distinct human "authors"/"traditions" as opposed to genre, register, or scribal-transmission effects within a single tradition remains a live methodological question among Hebrew linguists (the same kind of dispute already noted elsewhere in this database regarding linguistic dating methods generally).

SOURCE: Faigenbaum-Golovin, S., Kipnis, A., Bühler, A., Piasetzky, E., Römer, T., & Finkelstein, I. (2025). "Critical biblical studies via word frequency analysis: Unveiling text authorship." *PLOS ONE*, 20(6): e0322905.

URL: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0322905
